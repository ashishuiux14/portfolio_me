01

A snap, then a material

The usual toggle slides across in a fixed time, which is closer to metal than to clay but still not quite right. A real switch starts slowly against its spring, speeds up and stops at full speed against the end.

Compare
The usual · 150 ms ease

Why

A metal knob has no give, so all of its character is in timing. Accelerating into the end and stopping dead is what makes it read as heavy and precise rather than floaty.

Why the green doesn’t fade in

On the clay toggle the green floor fills as the knob moves. Here it lights the instant the knob hits the end, like a circuit closing. Halfway is still off.

02

It sinks when you press

Clay stretches under a finger. Metal can’t, so it travels down instead. Press and hold: the knob drops into its housing, its face catches a little less light and its shadow tightens.

Why

The press is answered before anything slides, just like the clay knob, but in the language of the material. A small drop, a slightly darker face and a tighter shadow are enough to feel the mechanism engage.

Why it doesn’t change shape

The knob keeps its exact outline at every moment. Any stretch, however small, would make it read as plastic or rubber instead of machined metal.

03

It resists, then commits

In detail. The aluminium curve is a power curve that ends at full speed: position = timedetent. At a detent of 1 it’s a straight line, a loose slider with no spring behind it. Higher values hold the knob back longer and release it faster, which is what makes a switch feel firm. The curve hits the end and goes flat with no overshoot, and the click is timed to that exact frame. The clay curve takes almost six times as long to come to rest.

04

Drag it and it fights back

Hold the knob and drag. It moves less than your finger, because the spring is pushing back. Get it past the middle and the spring takes over: it snaps to the end on its own, before you let go. Release short of the middle and it returns.

Why it moves on its own

Clay goes wherever the finger takes it. A switch has a point of no return, and once you cross it the outcome is decided. Showing that moment is what makes the drag feel mechanical.

Why there’s no stretch past the ends

Clay squashes against the wall. Metal simply stops. Dragging beyond the end does nothing at all, which is exactly what a hard material should do.

In detail. While dragging, the knob covers 60% of the distance your pointer does. At 50% of its travel it commits: the click plays, the light switches and the knob finishes the trip by itself. Released before that point, it slides home in 90 ms with a dull tap instead of a click, because no contact was made.
