A toggle made of clay

Claymorphism is usually treated as a look: puffy shapes and soft shadows. This study treats it as a material. If the knob is soft, it should give when you press it, stretch when it moves and settle like something with weight. Press one, hold it, drag it, let go.

01

A snap, then a material

Most toggles slide the knob across in a fixed time and stop. It works, and it could be made of anything. Clay should behave like clay.

Why

The material is the promise the interface makes. A soft, rounded knob that moves like a rigid slider breaks that promise, and people feel it even if they can’t name it.

Why not more bounce

The wobble has to end quickly. A toggle that keeps jiggling after you’ve moved on draws attention to itself instead of to the setting you just changed.

02

It gives when you press

Press and hold either toggle. The knob stretches toward the side it’s about to travel to, from whichever end it sits at.

Why

The press is answered the moment your finger goes down, before anything moves. The knob gets wider and flatter by the same amount, so it reads as the same lump of clay being pushed, not as something growing.

Why it stretches one way

The outer edge stays against the wall of the track, because the wall is in the way. Only the inner edge reaches out, which also hints at where the knob is about to go.

03

It overshoots, then becomes round again

The knob arrives with momentum, runs a few pixels past its resting place, flattens against the wall and springs back into a perfect circle.

In detail. The knob is a damped spring: a weight on a spring, released toward the other end. Elasticity sets how little the spring is damped. At 1 the curve meets its rest line and stops, like a firm material. Lower values let it shoot past and swing back. The track only allows 7 px of overshoot, so anything beyond that turns into squash against the wall instead of travel, and the knob’s height grows by the same proportion its width shrinks.

04

It waits while you hold it

While your finger is down, the knob stays stretched and ready. Drag it and it follows, with resistance at the ends. Let go past the middle and it carries on to the other side. Let go short of that and it springs back.

Why the knob follows the finger

Holding the stretched knob and pulling it is the most physical moment of the interaction. Letting the finger decide, and backing out by dragging home, makes the toggle feel held rather than triggered.

Why a tap still toggles by default

A tap is the most common way to use a switch, and it’s how keyboards and screen readers operate it. “Drag only” shows the stricter behaviour: a tap just squishes the knob and lets it go.

In detail. Movement under 4 px counts as a tap. Past that, the knob tracks the pointer one to one, and beyond the ends it moves at a quarter of the pointer’s speed, like clay being pulled against a wall. On release, the knob keeps the speed of your drag, so a flick throws it across and a slow drag eases it. It changes state once it has travelled 30% of the way.
