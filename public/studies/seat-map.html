Interaction study 06 · Seat map

Draw what you can book boldest

Most seat maps get their own hierarchy backwards. Booked seats are drawn as bold, empty outlines that look like open spaces waiting to be filled, while the seats you can actually choose are flat fills that look already taken. Architects solved this long ago: the line closest to you is drawn darkest and thickest, and everything further away gets lighter and thinner. This study applies that rule to a seat map.

What goes wrong before

The boldest marks are the booked seats: crisp empty outlines that look like open spaces. The seats you can actually book are flat fills that look occupied. Selected turns orange, a third, unrelated colour, so the legend has to be read every time.

What changes after

Available seats get the boldest line and the brightest colour. Your selection is a solid fill of that same colour, so it reads as “one of those, now mine”. Booked seats shrink back to thin grey outlines, still visible for orientation but clearly out of reach.

02

The line-weight rule, from architecture

In a section drawing, what the cutting plane passes through is drawn heaviest. Things seen beyond it get lighter and thinner the further back they are. Weight tells you distance, and distance tells you what matters.

Why it works on a seat map

A seat map is a drawing with a job: help you act. The seats you can act on are “closest” to you, so they get the heavy line. The booked ones are scenery and step back.

Why weight, not only colour

Line weight and size survive bad screens, sunlight and colour blindness. Colour alone doesn’t. Section 04 tests this.

03

Someone is sitting there: you

A selected seat gets a small human figure. It turns an abstract fill into “this is where you’ll be”, and with a group it shows at a glance how many people you’ve seated.

Select a few seats
Why a person

Booking for four, people count seats against heads. One figure per selected seat makes that count instant, and it’s the most literal possible answer to “is this mine?”.

Why it drops in

The figure settles into the seat with a short drop, like someone sitting down. It’s the only motion on the map, so it’s also the confirmation that the tap registered.

04

The greyscale test

Turn the colour off. On the usual map, the pale blue and the orange collapse into similar greys, and the only bold marks left are the seats you can’t book. With the line-weight rule, every state still reads, because it was never carried by colour alone.

05

Every seat has a voice

Click a seat, then use the arrow keys. A screen reader hears the row, the number, the price and the state, including seats that are already booked, so the layout makes sense without seeing it.

Why booked seats stay focusable

Skipping them would make the rows jump unpredictably. Landing on one and hearing “booked” tells you where the gaps are.

Why the price is in the name

Price is the second question after “is it free?”. Hearing it with the seat saves a trip to a summary somewhere else.
