Date Range Picker Study
Interaction study 01 · Date range picker

Two clicks that shouldn’t need thinking

A date range picker shows up in nearly every product: reports, bookings, invoices, leave requests. The usual version works, and it still makes people count squares, fight a date mask and guess what the second click will do. This study starts from the plain version and adds one decision at a time, each with its reason and the alternative I rejected.

Ashish DixitSenior Product Designer8 decisions · 10 min
The finished component · live

01

Start with what already works

Two native date inputs. They are accessible and cost nothing to build, so every layer after this has to earn its place over them.

The plain version

Try setting the end before the start.

What it gets right

Native inputs work with every screen reader, follow the person’s locale and need no maintenance. If a custom picker can’t beat them on speed and clarity, it shouldn’t exist.

What it misses

Two separate pop-ups that know nothing about each other. No sense of how long the span is. Nothing stops an end date before the start date.

02

Name the parts

Before changing anything, agree on what each piece is called. The rest of this study uses these names.

03

Show the range before it’s chosen

After the first click, the calendar previews what the second click would select. The 6th is already picked here. Move the pointer over a later date.

Interactive
Why

Without a preview, people choose the end date blind and then check the result by counting. With it, the second click becomes a confirmation of something they can already see.

Why not highlight only the hovered day

A single highlighted day tells you where the pointer is. It doesn’t tell you what you are about to select, which is the only question that matters at that moment.

In detail. The preview is the same band as a finished range at half strength, so committing reads as the preview settling, not as something new appearing. The day under the pointer gets a ring instead of a fill, because it isn’t chosen yet. Arrow keys drive the same preview, so keyboard users get it too.

04

When the second click lands before the first

Someone picks the 18th, then clicks the 9th. The calendar can respond in three reasonable ways. Choose one and click a date before the 18th.

Why start over

A click before the start almost always means the first click was wrong. Treating it as the new start matches that intent, and the preview immediately shows what the next click will do.

Why not swap, why not an error

Swapping quietly builds a range out of a mistake, and the person has to notice it and undo it. An error blames someone for a reasonable click and hands them a fix the calendar could have made.

05

Let people type, and say how it was read

People who already know their dates are faster on the keyboard than in any calendar. The field accepts the way people actually write dates and shows its interpretation as they type.

Interactive · try one
Why

Typing is the fastest path for people who repeat a task and the main path for many keyboard and screen reader users. Showing how the input was read is what makes forgiving parsing safe.

Why not a mask like MM/DD/YYYY

A mask accepts one format, breaks on paste and confuses anyone who writes the day first. It moves the work of formatting onto the person instead of the software.

In detail. With no year typed, the field assumes the current year. When the end would land before the start, as in 12/24 - 1/2, the end rolls into the next year and the readout says so. Numbers are read month first, and the readout flags it whenever both numbers could be a month. Impossible dates such as feb 30 are refused with an example of what works.

06

Same component, opposite rules

A report looks back. A booking looks forward. The presets, the dates you can’t pick and the unit of the summary all flip with it.

Interactive
Why

Presets are where most selections happen, so they have to match the job. Disabling impossible dates prevents an error instead of reporting one after the fact.

Why not one universal preset list

“Last 30 days” on a hotel search, or “This weekend” on a revenue report, tells the person the component wasn’t designed for their task.

In detail. “Last 7 days” includes today, so it starts six days back. Getting that off by one is a classic reporting bug. A booking counts nights, which is the number of days minus one, because that is what the price is based on. A same-day booking is refused with a reason.

07

One band, not eighteen squares

How a range is drawn decides whether it reads as one thing or many. This range crosses three week rows and a month boundary.

Interactive
Why

A continuous band reads as a single span with two ends. Separate squares read as many individual choices, and the start and end get lost among them.

Why not squares

Squares are easier to build, since every day is styled on its own. That saving goes to the engineer, and the cost goes to everyone who reads the calendar.

In detail. The band sits behind each day and rounds off wherever a week row breaks or a month ends, so the eye can follow it from one row to the next. The start and end sit on top as solid circles of equal weight, which makes them read as the two handles they are.

08

Every move has a keyboard twin

Everything the pointer can do, the keyboard can do, and a screen reader hears what changed. Click a date, then use the keys.

In detail. Only one day sits in the tab order at a time and the arrows move focus, so a single Tab leaves the calendar. Announcements are polite, so they never cut off what the screen reader is already saying, and they lead with the result: the date, then what it means for the range.

What I’d measure after shipping

  • Time to a finished rangeFrom opening the field to a complete selection, split by typed, clicked and preset.
  • Edits right after selectingA range changed within seconds of being set means the preview or presets missed.
  • Preset use per contextPresets nobody picks get removed; ranges people build by hand often become new presets.
  • Errors from typed inputEvery refused entry is a format to support or a hint to rewrite.

Rejected along the way

  • A slider for the rangeFine for prices, too imprecise for dates, and hard to use with a keyboard.
  • Closing the panel on the second clickRight for a booking. In a report, people adjust a range several times, so the panel stays open.
  • Two months side by side on phonesOne month at full width, with presets in a scrolling row above it.
  • Showing days from neighbouring months in the gridThey make a band look like it continues into dates that aren’t selected.
